The one-screen version

If you read nothing else: India's securities problem in 2025 is not awareness — it's conversion, trust, and knowledge depth. The whitespace is the affluent, advice-dependent, vernacular, non-metro household that already knows mutual funds exist but hasn't been walked across the line.

Seven things drive everything downstream in this report, and each maps to a product or GTM lever:

  1. Awareness is solved; conversion isn't. 63% aware → 9.5% invested. The funnel leaks at "I don't know how to start," not "I've never heard of this."
  2. Knowledge is shallow even among investors. Only ~36% of investors have high/medium knowledge; ~50% are low-knowledge. This is a guided-journey opportunity, not a self-serve one.
  3. The intender pool is rural and affluent. ~50% of intenders are rural; NCCS A/B = ~72% of intenders; Gen Z + Millennials dominate. Non-metro affluent is the growth frontier.
  4. Trust is intermediated and social. Friends/family (59%) and finfluencers (56%) are the top information sources; ~55% of clients fully depend on an intermediary.
  5. Risk aversion is structural. 80% of households prioritise capital preservation; even 71–74% of equity/MF investors prefer low risk. Products must lead with safety framing.
  6. Friction is the silent killer. "Easy process/access" is the #1 motivator (73%); post-investment, complexity/inconvenience hits 66–84%. Onboarding and ongoing UX are conversion levers, not hygiene.
  7. The premium stack (PMS/AIF/SIF) barely registers. AIF awareness 6%, penetration <1%, concentrated in NCCS A metros. It's a relationship-led, narrow-funnel game — not a mass-acquisition one.
Strategic read · the headline for FundsIndia

FundsIndia's structural position — an advice-led MFD/RIA platform with RM relationships — is aimed squarely at the single biggest gap the survey exposes: the aware-but-not-invested, low-knowledge, advice-dependent household. The pure-execution discount players have harvested the self-directed metro investor. The survey says that cohort is small and already converted. The unconverted majority explicitly asks for "trusted financial advice and guidance" and "someone to show me how to start" — which is a human-plus-digital wedge, not a zero-touch app wedge.

The launch question is therefore less "which instrument" and more "which guided journey for which cohort, delivered through which blend of RM, content and platform." The instrument layers slot into that.

01

Market structure & the participation gap

The macro backdrop is genuinely favourable — but the survey's defining finding is the chasm between a country that knows about markets and one that invests in them.

Market lensInvestor lensRM/IFA lens

The decade of formalisation is real. Nominal GDP went ₹124.7L cr → ₹330.7L cr (2015–25); MF AUM ₹12L cr → ₹79L cr; BSE market cap ₹101L cr → ~₹470L cr. Adult account ownership hit 89% (2024); UPI runs ~18,587 cr transactions/yr; Aadhaar e-KYC has made digital onboarding the default. Video KYC, CAS, micro-SIPs, and execution-only MF platforms have structurally lowered the entry barrier.

And yet penetration is 9.5%. Of 33.72 cr households, only ~3.21 cr hold any securities product. Only 8.5% hold a demat account at all — and 38% of those are dormant. Awareness is broad and shallow; participation is narrow and concentrated.

Where penetration concentrates. Urban 15% vs rural 6%. Top-9 metros 23%; 10–40L towns 16%; 5–10L towns 14%. NCCS A 24% vs NCCS C/D/E 2%. Postgraduates 27% vs ≤10th-grade 3%. Salaried 23% vs agri 4%. Men 11% vs women 7%. Delhi (21%) and Maharashtra (17%) lead states. Participation mirrors India's socio-economic geography almost perfectly — which means the addressable premium market is small, identifiable, and already contested.

Strategic read · where the market actually is

Two distinct games sit inside this data. Game A (depth): the metro NCCS-A household is already invested and over-served — here the contest is share-of-wallet, premium products (PMS/AIF/SIF) and advice quality, not acquisition. Game B (breadth): the 10–40L and 5–10L town, NCCS A/B household at 14–16% penetration is the genuine expansion frontier — high enough income to invest, low enough penetration to grow, and critically under-served by advice.

For FundsIndia, Game B is the structurally attractive launch target: it rewards a trusted, advice-led brand and a vernacular, guided-onboarding product more than it rewards a price war. The metros are where you defend; the 5–40L towns are where you grow.

The investor funnel & conversion math

The survey segments the entire 33.72 cr household universe into a clean funnel. Reading it as a funnel — rather than as separate chapters — is where the GTM targeting comes from.

The intender cohort is the single most actionable segment in the report. Among aware non-investors, 22% intend to invest within 12 months. Their profile inverts the usual assumption:

  • ~50% are rural; top-9 metros are only ~12% of intenders.
  • NCCS A + B = ~72% of the intender base — affluent, not mass-bottom.
  • ~70% male (a large, explicit female whitespace).
  • Gen Z 56% + Millennials 35% — young, digital, aspirational.
  • Top intender states: UP (11%), Maharashtra (10%), Tamil Nadu (9%), West Bengal & Bihar (8% each).

The catch, and it's a big one: only 17.2% of non-investors have high/medium knowledge. Even in the highest-intent segments, <25% are knowledgeable. Intent is running well ahead of understanding — these are people about to make "blind bets" unless someone guides them.

Strategic read · the launch beachhead

This is the most important slide for targeting. The intender is affluent + young + non-metro + advice-hungry + knowledge-poor. That is almost a literal spec sheet for an advice-led platform's ideal acquisition target. A discount app converts the knowledgeable self-starter; it cannot serve someone who is about to make a blind bet and is actively asking to be shown how.

Concretely for a launch: a guided first-investment journey — vernacular, low-ticket (micro-SIP), with a human RM or a high-trust assisted layer at the "how do I start" moment — is pointed directly at converting the 22% intender pool. The female intender gap (~30% of the pool) is a defensible, under-contested sub-segment worth an explicit play.

Caution flag · don't over-read rurality

"50% of intenders are rural" is a share-of-intenders figure, not a penetration or revenue figure. Rural intenders are numerous but lower-ticket and costlier to serve. The commercially sharp cut is NCCS A/B intenders in 5–40L towns — the report repeatedly notes most intenders cluster in cities of 10L+. Treat "rural affluent" as a brand/funnel-top signal, and "tier-2/3 town affluent" as the revenue target.

03

Investor behaviour: triggers, fears, risk appetite

What pulls people in, what blocks them, and the deep risk-aversion that constrains every product framing decision.

Why non-investors stay out (barriers)

  • Complexity & information gaps — 74% ("don't know how products work / how to start")
  • Risk & return concerns — 73% (fear of losing money tops every product)
  • Trust & transparency — 51% (acute for F&O / derivatives)
  • Cost — 26% · External influence — 18%

What would pull them in (motivators)

  • Easy process / access — 73% (simple onboarding, friendly platform)
  • Education & information — 62% (how it works + success stories)
  • Cost efficiency — 61% (lower minimums, lower fees)
  • Trust & assurance — 58% (recommendations + trusted advice)

Triggers that convert investors: high growth potential (72%), income generation (58%), diversification/risk mitigation (51%), and convenience/ease (37%). Notably, behaviour drifts toward discipline after entry — goal-based reasoning rises from 18% → 23% once people are in. People enter on aspiration and stay on structure. F&O investors are the exception: disproportionately motivated by quick gains.

The risk-aversion ceiling — % preferring low risk

All households · 80%
Silver Gen · 85%
MF/ETF investors · 74%
Stock investors · 71%
Gen Z (most risk-open) · 79% still low-risk

The risk story is the most under-appreciated constraint in the report. Even people who already own equities and mutual funds overwhelmingly self-describe as low-risk. Only ~9–10% of MF/equity investors are comfortable with high risk. A homemaker quote captures the funnel-top emotion precisely: "I've heard about stocks and mutual funds… but I don't know how to start. The process seems complicated, and I'm afraid of losing my savings."

Strategic read · product framing & sequencing

Three direct consequences for launch design. (1) Lead with safety, not upside. Even your equity buyers are loss-averse — "managed risk," "professional management," "goal-based" framing converts better than return-maximisation messaging. The report shows MFs are perceived as the "balanced middle" and are the natural entry product; that perception is an asset to lean on.

(2) The motivator stack is a product roadmap: easy process (onboarding UX) → education (guided content) → low minimums (micro-SIP) → trusted advice (RM/RIA layer). FundsIndia can credibly own all four; a discount broker can only own one.

(3) Sequence the ladder. Entry via MF/SIP (safety + low ticket), graduate to direct equity/ETF, and reserve PMS/AIF/SIF for the demonstrated-affluence, advice-deep tail. Don't launch a premium product into a cohort that hasn't crossed the trust threshold.

The finfluencer finding is striking and double-edged. 93% of investors rate finfluencers moderately-to-highly credible; 62% make at least some investment decisions on their recommendations. YouTube (91%), Instagram (64%), Facebook (61%) lead. Among non-investors, social media is the #1 awareness channel (56%), with TV still strong (40%) for credibility, especially for MFs.

Meanwhile official/regulator channels sit at the bottom (21%), and only 6% of households know SEBI's grievance mechanism exists. There's a trust vacuum between "social media hype" and "official-but-invisible."

Strategic read · content as an acquisition channel

This is where a regulated, advice-led brand has an unusual opening. Investors trust finfluencers but the report's subtext (and SEBI's whole regulatory thrust) is that this trust is fragile and partly misplaced. FundsIndia can occupy the "credible finfluencer-grade content, but accountable and advice-backed" position: vernacular YouTube/Instagram explainers, success-story formats (explicitly requested by non-investors), and short-form video — which the survey names as the single most-preferred education format (videos 80%, social posts 69%).

Two concrete levers: (1) build the top-of-funnel on YouTube/IG education in Hindi + regional languages, not English (only 5% prefer English content); (2) treat the RM/IFA as the "trusted human" the survey says 58% of non-investors want — the conversion layer beneath the content layer. Content earns attention; the RM closes the trust gap that social media can't.

For MFDs and RIAs alike, 61% of clients are fully dependent on the intermediary for advice and execution. Even at the PMS/AIF end, roughly half the clients hand over comprehensive consultation. This is the empirical backbone of the advice-led thesis: the Indian investor, across the wealth spectrum, is not a self-directed actor.

What clients ask intermediaries for (operational)

  • SEBI-mandated KYC guidance — 64%
  • Linking PAN/Aadhaar/bank — 58%
  • Updating personal details — 58%
  • Online account opening / digital onboarding — 56%
  • Risk profiling & investor categorisation — 44%

Top investor complaints (intermediary-reported)

  • Technical issues with platforms/apps — 47%
  • Mis-selling / poor product suitability — 41%
  • Non-updation of client details — 38%
  • Poor/no response from intermediaries — 34%
  • Grievance redressal delays — 30%

How intermediaries actually reach clients: for acquisition, WhatsApp (54%) → tele-calling (35%) → door-to-door (25%). For servicing existing clients, WhatsApp (78%), phone (74%), in-person meetings (69%), email (54%). This is a high-touch, relationship-heavy, WhatsApp-native distribution reality — not a self-serve one.

Intermediary profile skews experienced: 70% have 9+ years' tenure; 22% manage 1,000+ clients. They handle MF/ETF (91%), stocks (51%), F&O (25%), corp bonds (23%), AIF (13%), REITs (11%). The newer asset classes barely feature in the distribution base.

Strategic read · the RM model is the moat

This chapter is, in effect, a validation of FundsIndia's core model. When 61% of even RIA clients are fully advice-dependent, the platform that wins is the one that makes the RM/advisor more productive, not the one that removes them. Three product implications:

(1) Build for the assisted journey. The most-requested client help is operational (KYC, onboarding, account ops). Tooling that lets an RM resolve these in-flow — co-browse onboarding, pre-filled KYC, status visibility — directly attacks the #1 complaint (technical/platform friction) and the #1 motivator (easy process).

(2) Mis-selling at 41% is both a risk and a wedge. A suitability-first, RIA-grade advice layer (fee-based, fiduciary framing) is a differentiator precisely because the market is full of suitability complaints. This is where the RIA side of FundsIndia's licence becomes a brand asset, not just a compliance box.

(3) WhatsApp is the channel, not an afterthought. 78% servicing / 54% acquisition. Any RM enablement or client-comms feature that isn't WhatsApp-first is mis-aligned with how this market actually transacts.

Caution flag · RIA/PMS/AIF sample is thin & self-selected

The intermediary base is 81% MFD and explicitly "not indicative of overall market incidence." RIA (10%), PMS (5%) and AIF (7%) cells are small (e.g. PMS n=69, AIF n=89). Read PMS/AIF/RIA findings as directional, not precise. The MFD signal is robust; the premium-tier signal is suggestive.

MF / ETF — the engine room

Highest awareness (53%) and penetration (6.7%) of any securities product; perceived as the "balanced middle" on the risk-return map; the natural first product. 67% of those aware of MF or stocks are aware of both. Metro MF penetration 18% vs rural 4%. MFs are explicitly the product investors research most deeply (fund types, AUM, manager track record). This is the acquisition workhorse — every entry journey should start here, ideally via micro-SIP to clear the cost barrier.

Strategic read · MF/ETF

Own the entry. A vernacular, guided SIP onboarding aimed at intenders is the highest-volume, lowest-risk launch. ETFs are a cost-efficiency answer to the 61% who cite fees/minimums — but the survey gives no evidence of independent ETF demand; position ETF as a low-cost passive on-ramp inside the MF journey, not a standalone hero product.

PMS & AIF — the affluent tail

Both sit below 1% penetration. AIF awareness is just 6%, concentrated almost entirely in NCCS A metro households — the report calls them "premium, high-ticket… highly niche and largely inaccessible." In the intermediary chapter, PMS and AIF clients are ~50% fully advice-dependent, and only 5–13% of intermediaries even deal in them. There is no mass-market signal here, and the report doesn't pretend there is.

Strategic read · PMS/AIF

Do not treat these as launch-acquisition products. They are retention and share-of-wallet instruments for the already-affluent, already-trusting client — the graduation tier of an existing relationship. The economics work through RM depth and ticket size, not funnel volume. For FundsIndia, the right posture is a curated, advice-gated PMS/AIF shelf offered to the top of the existing book, not a marketed top-of-funnel launch. The thin survey base reinforces this: there's no demand wave to ride, only relationships to deepen.

SIF — the greenfield the survey can't see

SIF does not appear anywhere in the survey instrument set — it post-dates the survey's product taxonomy. So there's no awareness, penetration, or demand data to lean on.

Strategic read & caution · SIF

Treat SIF as category-creation, not demand-capture. The survey's adjacent signals are the relevant ones: (a) there's a real "mass-affluent between MF and PMS" cohort (NCCS A/B in 5–40L towns) that is under-served by both ends; (b) that cohort is deeply advice-dependent and risk-averse. SIF's strategic fit is as the graduation product for the maturing MF investor who has outgrown plain mutual funds but isn't a PMS-ticket client — exactly FundsIndia's Game-B cohort a few years on. But because there's zero demand evidence, a SIF launch must be RM-led and education-first, and should be sequenced after the MF acquisition engine is proven, not alongside it.

Dormancy, lapsing & retention

40% of investors are dormant. Acquisition without retention design leaks the funnel you just filled — and the survey is unusually specific about why people disengage.

6/8

Dormancy drivers: poor performance (87% — lower returns, volatility, losses), external influences (75% — negative peer experiences, need for funds), market sentiment (31%). Lapsing drivers: poor performance (84%), change in personal goals (74% — peer experiences, urgent fund needs), cost/fees (30%), market sentiment (28%).

Post-investment friction compounds it. Among investors, lack of knowledge/information actually rises after entry (47% → 69%): unclear statements, no timely fund-manager updates, difficulty tracking performance. Intermediaries confirm complexity/inconvenience jumps to a top-3 post-investment problem (84%), driven by managing multiple investments, technical glitches, and clunky redemptions.

Strategic read · retention is a product, not a campaign

Two high-leverage moves fall straight out of this. (1) Engineer against performance-panic. Since 84–87% of disengagement traces to performance disappointment in a population that is structurally loss-averse, proactive, plain-language portfolio communication — "here's what's happening and why, here's the plan" — is a retention feature. The survey explicitly flags "no clear/timely updates from fund managers" as a top post-investment gap; closing it is a differentiator.

(2) Build a re-activation engine. 6 of 8 lapsers want back in, and 40% of investors are merely dormant, not gone. A targeted, RM-assisted win-back motion — especially around goal re-anchoring (the #2 lapse reason is "change in personal goals") — is cheaper than net-new acquisition and is sitting in your own book. For FundsIndia, dormant-client re-activation may be the highest-ROI launch-adjacent program of all.

08

Consolidated launch implications

Pulling every layer together into a sequenced view. These are interpretations for FundsIndia, not survey findings — weighted by where the data is strongest.

Tier 1 · Now

Guided MF/SIP acquisition for tier-2/3 affluent intenders

Strongest data support. Vernacular, video-led top-of-funnel; micro-SIP entry; RM/assisted "how to start" conversion. Targets the 22% intender pool where it's commercially densest (NCCS A/B, 5–40L towns). Female-intender sub-play as a defensible wedge.

Tier 1 · Now

Dormant-book re-activation

40% of investors dormant; 6/8 lapsers want back. Goal re-anchoring + proactive performance comms. Highest ROI, lowest CAC, uses the existing book. Pure retention-as-product.

Tier 2 · Next

RM/IFA productivity & suitability layer

61% of clients fully advice-dependent; 41% mis-selling complaints. WhatsApp-first servicing, co-browse onboarding, fiduciary/RIA-grade suitability as brand asset. Makes the advisor better rather than removing them.

Tier 2 · Next

ETF as low-cost passive on-ramp

Answers the 61% fee/minimum-sensitivity, but no standalone ETF demand in the data — embed inside the MF journey, don't hero it.

Tier 3 · Later / gated

Curated PMS/AIF shelf for the affluent tail

<1% penetration, NCCS-A metro only, thin survey base. Advice-gated graduation product for top-of-book; share-of-wallet, not acquisition.

Tier 3 · Later / gated

SIF as category creation

Zero demand data — greenfield. Sequence after the MF engine proves out; RM-led, education-first, aimed at the maturing mass-affluent MF investor.

The through-line. Every layer of this survey points the same direction: the Indian opportunity in 2025 is conversion and trust, not awareness; the under-served prize is the affluent, advice-dependent, vernacular, non-metro household; and the winning model is human-plus-digital guidance, not zero-touch self-serve. That is a description of FundsIndia's structural strengths. The launch strategy that follows the data is one that leads with a guided MF acquisition engine for the intender cohort, defends with an RM productivity and suitability layer, monetises the existing book through re-activation and graduation, and treats PMS/AIF/SIF as relationship-gated tiers rather than mass launches.

What the survey can't tell you — and where you'll need your own data — is unit economics by cohort, the true CAC of the rural-affluent intender, SIF/PMS demand elasticity, and the competitive response from the discount incumbents. The survey sizes and shapes the opportunity; it doesn't price it.
