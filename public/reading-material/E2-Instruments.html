The Instrument Entity

A master reference for what an investor can hold and transact. Iteration one covers the managed-fund instruments: Mutual Funds and Specialised Investment Funds.

What this document is

A reference view of the Instrument entity: what each instrument is, how it is priced and acquired, how it moves through its lifecycle, how it settles, what constrains its liquidity, what risk and disclosure regime governs it, and the thin tax character a distributor needs to carry. It is the second in the same family as the Investor reference and follows the same common-spine-plus-profile structure.

What it is not

Not a PRD and not a build plan. It carries no flow specifications. It also does not reproduce tax mechanics: the rate-and-holding-period detail lives in the Investor reference and is pointed to from here, not duplicated.

Scope of this iteration

In scope now: Mutual Funds, including index funds treated as a mutual fund variant, and Specialised Investment Funds (SIF). Both are pooled, AMC-managed, NAV-based, RTA-settled fund vehicles under the Mutual Fund Regulations, which is why they sit together.

Deliberately deferred: Bonds and Fixed Deposits are held for a later iteration to keep this document focused on the instruments in the immediate build. Equities, Insurance, and Lending are a separate exercise planned later. ETFs travel with Equities. REITs and InvITs are parked. Digital gold and crypto are permanently excluded as unregulated.

Relationship to the other references

Three boundaries keep this document from swelling into its neighbours. Tax appears here only as a per-instrument character tag; the rate table lives in the Investor reference. The order lifecycle (placed, routed, acknowledged, allotted, settled, failed, reversed) is described here only as far as each instrument bends it; the generic machinery belongs to the Order reference. Holding, nomination, and transmission are folio-and-investor concerns and live in the Folio reference, not here.

Part I
The instrument entity model

1.What an instrument is in this domain

An instrument is a thing that can be held and transacted, wrapped in a regulatory structure, carrying a pricing mechanic, a lifecycle of its own, and a tax character. Like the investor, it is best understood as a shared spine that each type bends.

The two instruments in this iteration share a great deal, because both are pooled investment vehicles run by an AMC under the Mutual Fund Regulations. They both issue units, strike a Net Asset Value, settle through a registrar, and support a common family of transaction types. What differs is the strategy freedom, the ticket size, the liquidity, and the risk regime. So the structure mirrors the Investor reference: a common fund-mechanics spine in Part II, then profiles in Part III that describe how each instrument modulates it.

Key principle

The instrument is not the system of record for what an investor holds. The AMC manages the scheme, the registrar (the RTA) maintains the unit register, and a custodian holds the underlying assets. A distributor reflects and reconciles that truth; it does not own it. This single fact, carried over from the Investor reference, decides most of the build-scope view in Part V.

2.The facet taxonomy

Each instrument profile is examined through the same facets, in the same order, so the two can be read side by side:

  1. Definition and regulatory wrapper: what it is and which framework governs it.
  2. Sub-types: the internal taxonomy.
  3. Pricing and valuation: how a unit is priced.
  4. Acquisition: how it is first bought.
  5. Transaction types and lifecycle: what actions it supports and how they bend the order lifecycle.
  6. Intrinsic events: lifecycle events the instrument generates on its own.
  7. Liquidity, lock-in and exit: how easily and on what terms it can be left.
  8. Risk and disclosure regime: how risk is classified and disclosed.
  9. Tax character: the thin classification tag, pointing to the Investor reference for rates.
  10. Counterparties and rails: who manages, records, and settles.
  11. Build-scope: what a distributor builds versus what the AMC and RTA own.

Part II
The common fund-mechanics spine

Six facets that both fund instruments share, described once here. The profiles in Part III state only their deviations.

4.The fund vehicle and the chain of custody

A fund pools money from many investors and issues units representing a proportional claim on a managed portfolio. Four parties sit behind every unit, and knowing which owns which truth is the foundation of everything downstream:

PartyOwns
AMC (asset management company)Manages the scheme and makes investment decisions; strikes the NAV.
RTA (registrar and transfer agent, CAMS or KFin)The unit register: the authoritative record of who holds how many units. The system of record.
CustodianHolds the underlying securities of the scheme.
TrusteeHolds the scheme in trust for investors and oversees the AMC.

A distributor originates the order and maintains its own reconciled view, but the unit register is the RTA's. This is why the document treats holdings as reflected rather than owned.

5.NAV and forward pricing

A fund unit is priced at its Net Asset Value (NAV). The NAV is simply the total value of everything the fund owns divided by the number of units on issue, worked out once at the end of each day. Two principles then matter for any transaction flow:

Forward pricing. An investor transacts at a NAV that is not yet known at the moment of placing the order. The applicable NAV depends on when the transaction is registered relative to a daily cut-off, and, for purchases, on when the money actually reaches the AMC.

The funds-realisation rule. For purchases, the applicable NAV is the NAV of the day on which the funds are realised and available to the AMC for utilisation, regardless of the order time or amount. So a purchase placed before cut-off but funded a day later receives the later day's NAV. Redemptions, by contrast, take the same-day NAV if registered before the redemption cut-off.

Cut-off times follow SEBI norms: for liquid and overnight funds, an earlier purchase cut-off (around mid-day) reflecting their money-market nature; for other schemes, an afternoon cut-off for both purchase and redemption. The exact times are operational detail for the Order reference; what matters here is that the cut-off plus realisation together decide the price, not the click.

6.Transaction types and the order lifecycle

Both fund instruments support a common family of transactions. The generic lifecycle states belong to the Order reference; here is how the fund transaction types map onto them.

TransactionWhat it does
Lumpsum purchaseA one-time buy at the applicable NAV.
SIP (systematic investment plan)A recurring purchase on a schedule, executed against a standing mandate.
SWP (systematic withdrawal plan)A recurring redemption on a schedule.
STP (systematic transfer plan)A recurring switch from one scheme to another within an AMC.
SwitchA redemption from one scheme and purchase into another, in one instruction.
RedemptionA sale of units back to the fund at the applicable NAV.

SIP and SWP are standing instructions and depend on a payment mandate (eNACH or UPI autopay). The mandate is the part the distributor orchestrates; the execution and allotment sit with the RTA. Mandate detail belongs in the Order and Folio references.

7.Holding form

Fund units can be held in statement-of-account (SOA) form, where the RTA's records are the holding and no demat account is needed, or dematerialised in a demat account. The form has consequences for nomination, transmission, and pledging, which are folio concerns. The instrument is largely indifferent to the form; the investor and the platform choose it. The holding-form decision and its downstream effects live in the Folio reference.

8.Settlement

Redemption proceeds settle to the investor's registered bank account on a scheme-dependent cycle: liquid and overnight funds the fastest, equity schemes a few days, with the exact cycle a property of the scheme type. Purchases settle as units credited to the folio once funds are realised, per the rule in Section 5. Because the firm never holds the money, settlement is between the investor's bank and the AMC through the RTA and the payment rail; the firm reflects it.

9.Distribution attributes

Three dimensions sit on top of any fund scheme and matter to a distributor specifically:

Direct versus Regular plan. Every scheme has both. A Direct plan carries no distributor and no commission and a lower expense ratio. A Regular plan embeds distributor commission in its expense ratio and pays the distributor a trail. A distribution business transacts Regular plans and earns the trail; this is the commercial spine of the model and the reason EUIN and ARN attribution, covered in the Investor reference, tags every transaction.

Growth versus IDCW option. Growth reinvests gains into NAV; IDCW (income distribution cum capital withdrawal, formerly dividend) pays out periodically and is taxable in the investor's hands at slab. The option chosen changes the cash-flow profile and the tax timing.

Expense ratio and exit load. The total expense ratio is the annual cost embedded in NAV. An exit load is a charge for leaving before a defined period. Both are scheme attributes the catalog must carry and the investor must see.

Part III
Instrument profiles

Each profile states only what the instrument changes about the common spine, through the same facet order.

10.Mutual Funds

Mutual Fund

Definition and regulatory wrapperA pooled investment scheme managed by an AMC under the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, issuing units at NAV. The most established and tightly regulated of the instruments in scope, and the baseline against which SIF is described.
Sub-typesSEBI's scheme-categorisation framework groups schemes into five families:
  • Equity: large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap, large-and-mid, flexi-cap, multi-cap, value, focused, sectoral or thematic, and ELSS (the tax-saving variant).
  • Debt: a wide set from overnight and liquid through ultra-short, low, short, medium and long duration, corporate bond, banking and PSU, gilt, credit risk, and others, distinguished by duration and credit profile.
  • Hybrid: aggressive, conservative, balanced advantage or dynamic allocation, multi-asset, arbitrage, and equity savings, distinguished by the equity-to-debt mix.
  • Solution-oriented: retirement and children's funds, which carry a lock-in tied to the goal.
  • Other: index funds and fund-of-funds. ETFs sit here in law but travel with Equities in this document's scope.
Pricing and valuationNAV, struck per valuation day, with forward pricing and the funds-realisation rule from Section 5.
AcquisitionEither at a New Fund Offer (the initial subscription at a fixed unit price) or, far more commonly, by ongoing purchase at the prevailing NAV through the RTA or an exchange order-routing platform.
Transaction types and lifecycleThe full common family: lumpsum, SIP, SWP, STP, switch, redemption. Closed-ended schemes restrict ongoing purchase and redemption to listing or maturity.
Intrinsic eventsIDCW payouts; segregated portfolios (side-pocketing) when an underlying debt holding is hit by a credit event; scheme mergers and changes in fundamental attributes (which trigger an exit window); and, for closed-ended schemes and FMPs, maturity.
Liquidity, lock-in and exitOpen-ended schemes offer daily liquidity at NAV. Constraints come from exit loads (a charge for early exit), ELSS (a three-year statutory lock-in tied to its tax benefit), solution-oriented lock-ins, and the closed-ended structure.
Risk and disclosure regimeEach scheme carries a riskometer rating and a documentation set (the Scheme Information Document, Key Information Memorandum, and Statement of Additional Information). Debt schemes additionally carry a potential risk class matrix expressing interest-rate and credit risk.
Tax character (tag only; rates in the Investor reference)The bucket follows composition: an equity-oriented scheme (at least 65 percent domestic listed equity) is taxed as equity; a specified mutual fund (more than 65 percent debt and money-market) is taxed at slab with no long-term benefit; the residual middle (gold, international, many hybrids and FoFs) takes 24-month, 12.5-percent treatment. ELSS carries the three-year lock and 80C eligibility. Residents face no redemption TDS; NRIs face at-source TDS. Rates and holding periods are in the Investor reference, not repeated here.
Counterparties and railsAMC (manages), RTA CAMS or KFin (unit register), custodian (assets), and the payment rail for the money leg. Order routing via the RTA path or an exchange platform.
Special attention and opensNone unusual. This is the reference instrument, and the place where the catalog and suitability surfaces do most of their work.

Index Fund (a mutual fund variant)

DefinitionA passively managed mutual fund that replicates an index rather than trying to beat it. Mechanically it is a mutual fund in every respect above: same wrapper, NAV, transaction family, settlement, and holding form. It is in scope precisely as an MF.
What differs in substanceThe management is passive, so the metric that matters is tracking error and tracking difference, not alpha, and the expense ratio is typically low. For suitability and comparison surfaces this changes what you show: a benchmark-relative tracking quality, not a manager's record.
The special handling that genuinely matters

Special attention

Index-fund tax character is not uniform; it follows whatever the index represents. An equity index fund tracking a domestic equity index is equity-oriented and taxed as equity. A debt or target-maturity index fund is, by composition, a specified mutual fund, so it is slab-taxed with no long-term benefit regardless of how long it is held. An international index fund falls into the residual bucket with 24-month, 12.5-percent treatment. So "index fund" must never be tagged with a single tax character in the catalog; the tag is derived from the underlying index, exactly as for any other scheme. This is the one place the "treat index as MF" rule needs a conscious carve-out.

Everything elseIdentical to the parent MF profile. No separate lifecycle, settlement, or risk-regime treatment is needed.

11.Specialised Investment Funds (SIF)

SIF is the newest instrument here, and the one where certainty is thinnest. What follows is current to mid-2026 and grounded in the SEBI framework and the early live funds, but the category is barely a year old, so several points are flagged rather than asserted. The structure is solid; the lived experience is not yet there.

Specialised Investment Fund

Definition and regulatory wrapperA SEBI-regulated product category created under the Mutual Fund framework, introduced by circular dated 27 February 2025 and effective 1 April 2025, positioned deliberately between mutual funds and Portfolio Management Services or AIFs.1 It gives AMCs strategy freedom that ordinary mutual funds do not have, principally long-short and derivative exposure, at an entry point well below the ₹50 lakh of PMS.
Who may launch itOnly eligible AMCs, by one of two routes: a sound-track-record route (a mutual fund operating at least three years with average AUM of at least ₹10,000 crore over the preceding three years), or an alternate route for AMCs bringing specialised expertise and meeting governance and fund-manager experience requirements.1 SIFs carry a brand identity kept separate from the AMC's mutual fund brand, for example Altiva, Magnum SIF, or iSIF.
Minimum investment₹10 lakh per investor, aggregated at PAN level across all SIF strategies of a single AMC, and excluding that AMC's regular mutual fund holdings. Accredited investors are exempt. Once the ₹10 lakh threshold is met across strategies, further top-ups can be as small as ₹10,000 or as specified in the offer document.2
Sub-typesThree categories and seven strategies, with only one strategy permitted per category per AMC to prevent proliferation:3
  • Equity: Equity Long-Short (at least 80 percent equity across caps, up to 25 percent unhedged short via derivatives); Equity Ex-Top 100 Long-Short (at least 65 percent outside the top 100 by market cap, the mid and small universe, up to 25 percent short); Sector Rotation Long-Short (80 percent across up to four sectors, up to 25 percent short).
  • Debt: Debt Long-Short; Sectoral Debt Long-Short, using exchange-traded debt derivatives for the short side.
  • Hybrid: Active Asset Allocator Long-Short (dynamic across equity, debt, REITs, InvITs, and commodities); Hybrid Long-Short (at least 25 percent each equity and debt).
Pricing and valuationNAV-based with daily pricing, like a mutual fund.
Transaction types, lifecycle and liquidityThis is where SIF departs most from a plain mutual fund. Equity SIF strategies have so far launched open-ended with daily redemption. Debt and hybrid strategies can be launched as interval funds, where redemption is permitted only in defined windows, commonly twice a week or once a month.4 So liquidity is strategy-specific and cannot be assumed daily.
Intrinsic mechanicsThe defining feature is the ability to take short positions, that is, to bet that a security will fall in price, on up to 25 percent of net assets using derivatives, alongside the ordinary long holdings. This is what lets the strategies try to make money, or limit losses, when markets fall, rather than only when they rise.
Risk and disclosure regimeA distinct, heavier regime than mutual funds, with its own offer documentation per strategy and explicit warnings that SIFs carry relatively higher risk including potential loss of capital, liquidity risk, and volatility. The separate branding requirement exists so investors do not mistake an SIF for the AMC's mainstream funds.
Tax character (tag only; rates in the Investor reference)SEBI's position is that an SIF is taxed like a mutual fund, by the orientation of its strategy, and notably without the fund-level taxation that Category III AIFs face.5 An equity-oriented SIF is taxed as equity, a debt-oriented one at slab. The derivative classification nuance is flagged below.
Counterparties and railsThe AMC under its SIF brand, the RTA, and a large-ticket payment path. Availability across third-party distribution platforms is still evolving; some AMCs route SIF through a dedicated platform rather than the ordinary mutual fund rails.
Special attention and red flags

Red flag: "long-short" does not guarantee shorting

SEBI permits 0 to 25 percent short exposure. A fund can carry the long-short label and hold no short positions at all. The label describes the permission, not the practice. Any suitability or comparison surface should reflect what a fund actually does, not what its name implies.

Red flag: no track record

The oldest SIFs are only months old as of mid-2026, and the category is still launching new strategies. There is not yet enough history to judge whether the downside-protection claims hold. This is a genuine, not rhetorical, caution for a mass-affluent or private-wealth recommendation.

Open: verify with Tax counsel

The headline "taxed like a mutual fund" is clear, but the bucket classification of a derivative-heavy long-short strategy is not obvious from the public material. Whether an equity long-short SIF reliably meets the equity-oriented test, given the derivative short overlay, should be confirmed for a specific strategy rather than assumed.

Open: verify with Compliance and the AMC

NRI and OCI eligibility for SIF is not settled in the public framework, and the large-ticket payment path is awkward for non-resident rails. Treat non-resident SIF access as unconfirmed.

Open: verify with the AMC and the offer document

The behaviour of the ₹10 lakh threshold once an investor's balance falls below it, for instance after partial redemption or a market fall, including whether any forced or restricted-redemption rule applies, is a scheme-and-framework detail I have not confirmed. Confirm against the strategy's offer document before designing a balance-threshold flow.

13.Glossary

Plain definitions of the financial terms a reader without a finance background may want unpacked. Widely familiar terms (mutual fund, SIP, exit load, demat, ELSS) are left out on purpose.

TermPlain meaning
Accredited InvestorA SEBI status for investors meeting set net-worth or income thresholds, which unlocks lighter rules such as the SIF minimum-ticket waiver.
AMCAsset Management Company, the firm that runs a fund and makes its investment decisions.
ARN / EUINThe distributor's registration number (ARN) and the servicing individual's number (EUIN), tagged to every transaction for attribution and commission.
DerivativesContracts whose value comes from an underlying asset, used here to take short positions.
Forward pricingYou transact at a NAV that is not yet known when you place the order, because it is struck later that day.
Funds-realisation ruleFor a purchase, the NAV that applies is the one for the day the money actually reaches the fund, not the day the order was placed.
IDCWIncome Distribution cum Capital Withdrawal, the periodic payout option once called dividend, taxable in the investor's hands.
Long-shortA strategy holding some securities expecting them to rise (long) while betting against others expecting them to fall (short).
NAVNet Asset Value, the per-unit price of a fund: the value of its holdings divided by the units on issue.
NFONew Fund Offer, the initial subscription window when a scheme first launches.
PMS and AIFPortfolio Management Service (₹50 lakh minimum) and Alternative Investment Fund (typically ₹1 crore minimum), higher-entry vehicles that sit above mutual funds and SIF.
RiskometerA standardised SEBI risk gauge shown on every scheme, running from low to very high.
RTARegistrar and Transfer Agent (CAMS or KFin), which keeps the official record of who owns how many units.
SID / KIM / SAIThe three mutual fund disclosure documents: Scheme Information Document, Key Information Memorandum, and Statement of Additional Information.
Short position (shorting)Betting that a security will fall in price, so the position profits if it does.
Side-pocketingWalling off a fund's troubled holding after a credit event so it does not drag down the rest.
SOAStatement of Account, holding fund units in the registrar's records without needing a demat account.
Specified Mutual FundA tax label for debt-heavy funds (more than 65 percent debt); their gains are always taxed at the investor's slab rate, with no long-term benefit.
STP and SWPSystematic Transfer Plan (move money periodically between schemes) and Systematic Withdrawal Plan (redeem periodically); the withdrawal-side mirrors of a SIP.
TERTotal Expense Ratio, the annual cost of running a fund, already deducted inside its NAV.
Tracking errorHow closely an index fund follows its index; lower is better.
Trail commissionThe ongoing commission a distributor earns on Regular-plan assets for as long as they stay invested.
