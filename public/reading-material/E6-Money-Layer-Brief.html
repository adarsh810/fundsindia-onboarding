The Money Layer

Everything the ledger session has to settle, in plain English: what is already decided and should not be reopened, the six decisions the session exists to make, and the five adjacent calls that belong in the same room.

Type Session brief · not a specification Date August 2026 For Head of New Platform, Head of Engineering, backend seniors Needed by Before feature code, October 2026

0What this is, and how to use it

The deepest gap in the platform's architecture is not an open decision in the register. It is a set of questions nobody has written down an answer to, and this document is those questions.

The ledger is drawn at full detail in the Architecture Companion, and that drawing is deliberately a strawman rather than a specification. The reasoning behind drawing it that way is worth repeating, because it explains what this session is for: it is faster to correct a drawn wrong answer than to specify from a blank page. Everything in the Companion's posting pipeline, account taxonomy, posting patterns and temporal policy exists to be argued with.

What makes this urgent rather than merely important is where it sits. The Build Strategy deliberately stops at principle altitude and defers all entity, event and ledger detail to Spec 1 and Spec 2. The Technical Architecture View names those two specs as its governing authority. Neither exists yet. So the chart of accounts, the posting rules, the event-to-posting mapping and the temporal policy currently have no written home anywhere in the canon, and feature code begins in October.

This is not something the Decisions Register can resolve

No decision number covers posting semantics or the account taxonomy. The register's related entries (D8, D37, D44, D46) are all closed with named follow-ups, meaning the shape is settled and only a value or a per-rail mechanic remains. The questions in section 2 below are a different kind of thing: they are unwritten rather than undecided, and they need a session rather than a ruling.

How to work it

Section 1 is the ground that should not be reopened. Section 2 is the six decisions, in the order the strawman raises them, each with the question in plain English, why it matters, the options, and what it blocks. Section 3 is the five adjacent calls that share the same room and the same people. Section 4 is the action list.

1Settled ground · do not reopen

Eight things hold regardless of how the session lands. They come from closed decisions and from the platform's foundational calls, and reopening any of them is a bigger conversation than this session.

Double-entry
Every posting balances. No single-sided entries.
Bitemporal
Two independent time axes: when the platform learned something, and when it was true. The system must always be able to answer both what it knew and when it was true.
Immutable
Nothing is edited or deleted. Corrections are compensating postings, with the original line surviving.
A projection, not the record
The ledger is a reconciliation projection of registrar truth. On any conflict the registrar wins and the ledger restates. Nothing a rail returns becomes truth until it has posted through these books.
No cash custody accounts
Because no investor money is ever held, no account in the chart of accounts represents money the firm holds. This follows from the no-pooling rule and is not negotiable.
Investor and folio altitude
Postings sit at investor and folio level. Household is a reporting projection and never a posting altitude. The roll-up reads; it never writes.
No advisory-fee posting
The distributor posture's only expression in the chart of accounts. The customer-money ledger records investment money movement only.
Out of V1 scope
No NPS unit accounts and no bond face-value accounts. Both are deferred instruments and neither earns a place in the V1 chart.

Two further constraints come from outside the ledger and bind it anyway. Holdings are derived from the ledger, never asserted alongside it, and never written directly by a rail. And reconciliation is batch in V1, with microbatch deferred to V2, which sets the tolerance for how stale a projection may be between cycles.

2The six decisions

Decision 1 · The account taxonomy

The question. What is the minimal set of accounts the ledger needs, and is the drawn set right?

The strawman. Four families are drawn. Units accounts, one per investor, folio and scheme, holding settled units and whether they are provisional. A money-in-flight mirror, tracking collection from initiated to realised. Charges and withholding legs, for exit loads, stamp duty and tax withheld at source. And a payout receivable for redemption proceeds in transit.

The sharpest sub-question, and the one to settle first

Are provisional units a separate account, or a state on the units account?

Provisional units are units the registrar has allotted before the purchase payment is confirmed realised. They exist on the register but the money is not yet certain, and if the payment later dishonours they have to be backed off.

If a separate account, the unwind is a clean transfer between two accounts and the balance in each is unambiguous at any moment, but every read of a holding has to know to look at two places and combine them correctly.

If a state on the units account, reads are simpler and the holding is one number, but the unwind becomes a state change on a balance rather than a movement between accounts, which is harder to express in a double-entry model without inventing a contra.

This answer decides how the payment-dishonour unwind posts, which is decision 4. Settle it first.

Why it matters. The account taxonomy is the thing every other decision is expressed in. Getting it wrong is not a bug that gets fixed later; it is a migration of every posting ever made.

What it blocks. Decisions 2 and 4 directly. Spec 2 entirely.

Decision 2 · The event-to-posting mapping

The question. Which events on the domain spine cause a posting, which are informational only, and how does the mapper behave when events arrive out of order or twice?

Three parts, and they are separable.

Part one: which events post. The order lifecycle emits seven events, each described in the canon as a distinct ledger-affecting fact: placed, routed to rail, acknowledged, allotted, settled, failed, reversed. Whether all seven post, or only a subset with the rest treated as informational, is the decision. The likely shape is that the money-and-units-moving events post and the routing and acknowledgement events do not, but that has to be stated rather than assumed, because ledger-affecting fact and causes a posting are not automatically the same thing.

Part two: the idempotency key per posting. Every money-moving call is idempotent by platform-wide rule. The ledger needs its own key so that the same event delivered twice produces one posting, and the key has to be derivable from the event rather than generated at posting time.

Part three is not a preference, it is forced by a vendor fact

The vendor documentation pass found that webhooks are in beta, with no ordering guarantee and no replay. So the mapper cannot assume it will see events in the order they happened, and cannot ask for a missed event again.

Two consequences the session has to settle explicitly. The mapper must be order-tolerant by design, meaning it can post a later event before an earlier one and still reach a correct balance. And reconciliation is the corrector rather than the backstop: the daily three-way match is not a safety net for a system that is otherwise reliable, it is a load-bearing part of how the ledger reaches truth.

Status sync is polling today with a push-ready seam. The mapper should be built for push semantics from the start, since the seam exists precisely so the swap is not a domain change.

What it blocks. The order saga in Stage 2, which is what the substrate gate tests.

Decision 3 · Money in flight, under no-pooling

The question. How are the money-in-flight legs named and structured so that they can never be read as the firm holding client money?

The background. Money moves from the investor's bank, through the payment aggregator, to the fund house. The firm is never in that path. But the ledger has to track the money's progress, because an order cannot be priced until funds are realised and cannot settle until payment is confirmed. So the ledger carries legs that mirror a movement it is not party to.

Why this is a naming decision and not only a modelling one

An auditor, a regulator, or an engineer joining in two years reads the chart of accounts to understand what the firm does with money. A leg named in a way that implies custody is a compliance problem even if the code behind it is correct, because the chart of accounts is a statement about the business.

The no-pooling rule is satisfied architecturally rather than procedurally. The chart of accounts should make that visible on its face, so that the answer to where does client money sit in your books is nowhere, by construction, and the account names say so.

What to settle. The naming convention for mirror legs. Whether they are grouped under a distinct family that is visibly not an asset of the firm. And what the reconciliation posture is when the mirror and the aggregator's own record disagree, since the aggregator's record is the authority on money in flight and the mirror is not.

Decision 4 · The unwind, per rail

The question. When a purchase payment dishonours after units have been provisionally allotted, exactly what does the platform post, and what does the rail tell it?

Why this is the costliest edge in the whole lifecycle. Units already exist on the registrar's register. The investor may have seen a holding. The register has to be corrected, and the platform's books have to follow with a compensating posting rather than a deletion. The canon is explicit that this window, between provisional allotment and confirmed realisation, is the most dangerous moment in the order lifecycle, and that the conservative design treats a purchase as final only when allotment and realisation agree.

Two things to settle. First, what the current rail's bounce-after-allotment flow emits, which is a vendor question rather than a design one and should be confirmed rather than inferred. Second, the exact compensating pattern, which depends on decision 1's answer about provisional units.

The part that recurs. This same question comes back at the second rail's integration in V1.5. Settle it as a named, reusable pattern rather than as a one-off for the current rail, so the second integration inherits the pattern rather than re-litigating it. Registered as D8, closed with a per-rail follow-up.

Decision 5 · The restatement policy

The question. When something turns out to have been wrong, when is it a restatement along the valid-time axis, and when is it a compensating correction?

The distinction, in plain terms. The ledger has two time axes. One records when the platform learned something; the other records when it was true. If the platform learns today that a price applied to a transaction three days ago was later revised, that is a restatement: the fact was always true, the platform just knew it wrongly. If a payment dishonours today on a purchase that allotted three days ago, that is a compensating correction: nothing about the past was wrong, something new has happened.

Both leave the original line in place. What differs is which axis moves and what the resulting statement says.

The guarantee that has to survive both

Statement reconstruction. The platform must be able to reproduce exactly what an investor was shown on a given date, and separately what the correct position for that date is now known to be. Those are two different questions and the ledger must answer both.

This is the practical expression of the bitemporal commitment. If the restatement policy is loose, the platform can end up unable to answer the first question, which is the one that matters in a dispute or an audit.

What to settle. The trigger list for each path, who may initiate a restatement, and whether restatement is a privileged operation with its own approval trail.

Decision 6 · Precision and batching

Two smaller questions that will cause real defects if left unstated.

Unit decimal conventions per registrar. Fund units are held to several decimal places, and the two registrars are not guaranteed to agree on rounding or precision. If the platform's convention differs from a registrar's, every reconciliation carries a rounding break that has to be distinguished from a real one. Settle the internal precision, the rounding rule, and the tolerance band within which a difference is not an exception.

Batch confirmation posting. When a registrar confirms several schemes in one batch, does the platform post one transaction or several? One transaction is cleaner to reconcile against the batch; several are cleaner to attribute to individual orders. The answer shapes the exception workflow, because a batch that partially fails behaves very differently under each.

3The five adjacent calls

These are register items rather than unwritten questions, but they need the same people in the same room and three of them are explicitly routed to this session.

Reconciliation cadence and freshness windows · D37

The direction is settled: design to the registrar and depository feed cycles, which are largely daily and batch, with buffers built in. What is not settled is the concrete windows and the reconciliation cadence. The values currently drawn on the architecture diagrams are design intent rather than confirmed numbers, and confirming them needs the feed providers as well as this room.

The related question worth settling alongside: how stale may a reflected copy be before an order is refused? The pre-check gate reads reflected external state and trusts it inside the agreed window, and the rare post-acceptance rejection is a defined edge. Where that window sits is a trade between refusing valid orders and accepting ones that will bounce.

The mandate ceiling breach · D44 follow-on

One mandate can back several plans within its ceiling. The open question is what happens when a new plan would push combined debits past that ceiling on a shared date: block it at setup, or allow it and let the debit fail on the day.

The leaning is block at setup, and the reasoning is that allow-and-fail knowingly feeds the consecutive-failure counter that ends in the plan being auto-cancelled at the registrar. Choosing to create a failure the platform can predict is hard to defend to an investor whose plan then gets cancelled. Record the outcome in the Mandate PRD.

The KYC downgrade detection sweep · D46 follow-on

The registries push nothing. A KYC status change is detected only by the platform polling for it. The cadence of that poll is unsettled, and it matters because the gap between a downgrade happening and the platform noticing is a window in which new business is accepted that should have been gated.

The consequence chain is worth stating: a downgrade means new orders and new plans are gated immediately, which is the platform's to do, while a running plan is paused only with the investor's consent. The longer the detection gap, the more orders slip through that first gate. Record the cadence in the Plans PRD.

Encumbrance feed mechanics · D28

Part-settled and deliberately parked. The scope ruling is made: V1 builds an encumbrance check into the claim and redemption flows, so a redemption against pledged or liened units fails its pre-flight check cleanly rather than being rejected later by the registrar. Only the feed mechanics, specifically how a lien on a statement folio differs from a pledge on a demat holding, are parked until lending enters scope. Nothing here blocks the session; it is listed so the seam is drawn with the parking in mind rather than closed off.

The three-book reconciliation during migration

Not a register item and easy to miss. During migration the same reconciliation engine runs three books per cohort as the cutover gate: the new ledger, the legacy book, and the registrar. That is a materially heavier posture than steady state, and it lands in V2 alongside the heaviest product build. The session should confirm that the reconciliation design can carry a third book without a rewrite, because discovering otherwise during migration is the worst possible time.

The one thing that makes this session worth holding

Every outcome must land in Spec 2 or in a decision record. A session that reaches good answers and writes none of them down leaves the platform exactly where it started, with the ledger's deepest questions living in people's heads. The canon's own doctrine says it plainly: the institution must not depend on oral tradition.
